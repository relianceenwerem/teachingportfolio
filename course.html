Rehumanizing Data

A semester that teaches technical writers to communicate data about people without erasing them.

  • Advanced writing
  • Interdisciplinary
  • Mixed-mode
  • 3 credit hours
Course description

An advanced writing course for students across disciplines (especially technical fields) that joins theory and practice around interdisciplinarity, academic identity, and the fundamentals of data literacy in industry. The course uses an integrative, triangular structure of class discussion, peer review, and individual and group projects, with each week combining readings, exercises, and discussion.

By the end, students can…

Student learning outcomes.

  • Develop critical digital and data literacy rooted in understanding datafication, with humanistic methods as a corrective.
  • Identify how dominant data paradigms are embedded in power dynamics and reflect ideologies.
  • Use feminist theory and the Feminist Data Manifest-No as an analytical lens for data projects.
  • Express the rhetorical choices behind data visualization, and evaluate how projects center or erase stories.
  • Articulate the ethical responsibilities of data collectors, curators, and communicators toward the people the data represents.
  • Adapt the data-humanism framework to projects within their own field.

The 16-week arc

Five movements across the semester.

The course moves from theory to hands-on making. Open each movement to see its weeks, readings, and milestones.

  1. 1

    1/11 · Introductions & goal-setting. Each student names their field and one way it uses data about people; a discussion post: share a number that represents you, then say what the number cannot capture.

    1/13 · Datafication & the Feminist Data Manifest-No. Close reading of the Manifest-No and an introduction to the five-dimension heuristic: care, embodiment, contextualization, small data, subjectivity. Read: Drucker (2021); Sutherland et al. (2023).

    1/20 · Data humanism & “data as capta.” Applying the five dimensions to a sample DH project. Read: Al-Hazwani et al. (2025); Drucker (2011).

  2. 2

    Week 3 · Workshop & peer review. Applying the heuristic to DH projects; peer review of drafts. Analytical Essay #1 due

    2/1 · Genre analysis. How does your discipline communicate data about people? Students bring a real document from their field and ask which conventions erase people. Read: D'Ignazio & Klein, Data Feminism, Ch. 6.

    2/3 · Treating data with care. Care vs. objectivity: students examine a public-health article and write a “care statement.” Read: Couldry & Mejias (2019); Lupi (2017).

  3. 3

    2/8 · Rhetoric & data visualization. Scale, color, framing, and omission as persuasion; three visualizations of one dataset: what does each argue, and for whom? Read: Dear Data; Drucker (2020).

    2/10 · Whose story does this chart tell? Students redesign a visualization to center a different community. Read: Noble (2018).

    Week 6 · Conference week. Individual conferences and a writing-center visit. Analytical Essay #2 due

    2/22–2/24 · AI & critical data literacy. What AI can and cannot do with data; forming cross-disciplinary groups for the multimodal project. Read: DeJeu (2024); Littlefield (2025).

  4. 4

    3/1–3/3 · Data cleaning & textual analysis. Sources for valid data; AI-assisted thematic analysis alongside traditional close reading. Read: Drucker (2021), Ch. 2; Braun & Clarke (2017).

    3/8–3/10 · Analysis, visualization & playful pedagogy. Voyant, Gephi, Power BI, Tableau; designing a data project playfully. Multimodal draft due

    Weeks 10–11 · Spring break & group conferences. Multimodal Project due

  5. 5

    3/29–3/31 · Ubuntu: “I am because we are.” Ubuntu as a counter-framework to datafication's individualism, and structuring accessible cross-disciplinary presentations. Read: Ewuoso & Hall (2019); Ramose (1999).

    Weeks 13–14 · Group presentations. Each group presents and receives structured peer feedback.

    Weeks 15–16 · What has this course changed? Students revisit their Week 1 “number that represents me” with new eyes. Reflection essay due

Artificial intelligence policy

AI, treated the way we treat data.

Powerful, imperfect, and never neutral.

We use Claude as a pedagogical tool. Students prompt it to build data artifacts, interrogate what it produces, and identify what it centers and erases, asking it to do things it cannot do well, and learning as much from the failures as the successes. Articulating what you want an AI to do with data forces you to name your own values, assumptions, and rhetorical choices. That naming is the course.

Experiment freely

Give Claude a dataset with no guidance, then with every course principle encoded, and compare. The omissions are the lesson.

Disclose honestly

Show your process: your prompts and Claude's responses are often the most interesting part of the work.

Think critically

Use AI to explore, but reflections, heuristic evaluations, and re-imagined narratives must be substantively your own.
