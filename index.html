Every dataset was once a room full of people. My classroom keeps them in the room.

The through-line

A classroom that refuses to treat people as data points.

I am a teacher of technical and professional communication with a background in the digital humanities. Across every course I teach, one commitment holds: data is never neutral, and neither is the way we communicate it. Numbers about human populations carry human stakes — and students in technical fields will spend their careers deciding whose stories those numbers tell.

So my pedagogy is built on four practices — engagement, universal design for learning, experimentation, and AI literacy — and organized around a five-part way of reading data with care: the same commitment this portfolio is designed to reflect.

One number, two stories

What datafication hides.

The same figure from the same public-health report, told two ways. Flip the switch — this is the move my whole course teaches students to make.

same data → different humanity

1,204

Excess heat-related deaths, Q3. County mortality exceeded seasonal projections by 18%. Records were disaggregated by age band and ZIP code; crude rate 42.7 per 100,000. Data complete, variance within model tolerance.

efficient. total. and it tells you nothing about a single person.

1,204

1,204 neighbors did not survive the summer. Each was someone's Tuesday phone call, someone's empty chair at dinner. Behind the 18% is a season the cooling centers closed early — and the blocks with the fewest trees and the oldest walk-ups lost the most people.

same number. now you can see who it holds.

The heuristic

Five ways of seeing data as human.

Students learn to read — and remake — any data project through five dimensions. Select one to see what it means and how it lives in my teaching.

Care

Treating data as a relationship, not a resource — honoring an obligation to the people a dataset represents.

In my classroom

Students write a care statement for a public-health article, naming their obligations to the people behind the figures before they analyze a single row.

Embodiment

Remembering that every data point was once a living body with a story numbers alone cannot hold.

In my classroom

Working from projects like the Middle Passage VR experience, students redesign a visualization from their own field to restore the bodies the statistics abstracted away.

Contextualization

Refusing the myth that numbers speak for themselves; situating data in the conditions that produced it.

In my classroom

A genre analysis of a real document from each student's discipline: which conventions make it easy to erase people, and which make it harder?

Small data

Valuing the intimate, hand-scaled, and particular over the totalizing sweep of “big data.”

In my classroom

Inspired by the Dear Data project, students compose data by hand at human scale — discovering how much meaning a single, carefully drawn week can hold.

Subjectivity

Making the interpreter visible; acknowledging that every visualization argues a position, for someone.

In my classroom

Three visualizations of one dataset, side by side: students ask what each one argues, and for whom — then take responsibility for their own choices.
