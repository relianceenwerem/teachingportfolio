Teaching Philosophy

An inclusive learning model built on engagement, universal design, experimentation, and AI literacy, aimed at access, representation, and community.

One of the hardest things I have done is teach a 6 p.m. undergraduate class, mid-winter.

Some students are fatigued from a full day of classes; some are just off work; everyone is thinking about the warmth of their beds, since it has been dark since five. And yet that class became one of my most memorable accomplishments: the experience that turned an abstract teaching philosophy into a solid one.

My teaching style is anchored on an inclusive learning model built on engagement, universal design for learning (UDL), interdisciplinary experimentation, and AI literacy. As a teacher of technical and professional communication with a background in digital-humanities methods, my teaching rests on these four practices that work together to reach each student. These practices took shape over the course of teaching that class.

How the model works.

Engagement, universal design, experimentation, and AI literacy are not separate techniques. Together they are what turned that tired, dark-by-five room into an alert and participating one.

Engagement

On the first day I invite students to ask me “stupid” questions, because the questions people are genuinely confused about, but afraid will sound dumb, are exactly the ones I want to hear. I make room for interactive, randomized questions during every lesson.

My syllabus flips the classroom: readings come before class so our time together is discussion and practice, where students connect their own backgrounds to the material.

students said the invitation to ask questions helped them pay closer attention.

Universal Design for Learning

Diverse students have diverse bodies and abilities. I advance UDL through multimodality: providing multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression so every student has a way in.

In practice that means accessible slides for every class, multiple platforms for composition and play, and a habit of foregrounding students' own goals for the course.

Experimentation

I give students room to work in and with emerging technologies across their fields, grounded in digital-humanities methods and principles of inquiry, an ethics of care, and social justice.

Because my classes draw students from many majors, assignments and group projects are built to hold interdisciplinary, multidisciplinary, and transdisciplinary perspectives at once.

AI Literacy

AI is not banned from my classroom. Used from a humanistic lens and under guidance, it has expanded my own creativity and made coding accessible, and TPC and DH practitioners need real AI literacy to explain its risks and its shifting roles.

So my activities are designed to make students critical users of AI: interrogating what it produces rather than accepting it.

The result

Participation over performance.

Because the syllabus accommodates students' interests, fosters an inclusive and playful pedagogy, and offers a guided approach to AI, students tend to excel despite challenges like fatigue. Grades primarily reflect participation (the flexible, active, collaborative work of showing up and contributing), while rubric engagement shapes the major assignments. Students leave with digital and critical-digital literacy grounded in humanistic principles: interpretive knowledge and an ethics of care they can carry into any field.

After a routine observation, my supervisor noted that my students were surprisingly alert for an evening class.
